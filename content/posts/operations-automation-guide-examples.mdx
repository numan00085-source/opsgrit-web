Operations is the work that keeps a business running: paying suppliers, hiring and onboarding people, handling requests, moving data between systems, keeping tools secure and producing the reports that managers rely on. Much of it is repetitive, follows rules and passes through several people and tools. That is exactly the kind of work software does well.

**Operations automation** is the practice of handing that repeatable work to software, so people spend their time on exceptions, judgment and improvement. This guide explains what it covers, gives twelve concrete examples, shows how to choose a first project and offers a five-step start that does not put the existing process at risk.

## What operations automation actually is

Three ingredients appear in almost every case:

1. **A trigger.** Something starts the work: an email arrives, a form is submitted, a date passes, a record changes.
2. **Rules or logic.** A set of steps decides what happens: check a field, compare a number to a limit, route to a person.
3. **Actions.** Software does the work: creates a record, sends a message, updates a system, files a document.

AI can be added where the work involves language or documents, such as reading an invoice, summarizing a ticket or drafting a reply. But a lot of useful automation needs no AI at all. A well-designed rule that routes requests to the right person saves hours, costs almost nothing and is easy to understand. For the difference between fixed workflows and AI agents, see [AI agents vs workflows in operations](/blog/ai-agents-vs-workflows-operations).

## Twelve examples of operations automation

### Finance

1. **Invoice capture and routing.** Read an incoming invoice, check it against a purchase order and send it to the right approver based on amount. See [AI invoice processing for accounts payable](/blog/ai-invoice-processing-accounts-payable).
2. **Approval reminders.** Nudge approvers when an invoice or expense report has waited too long, and escalate to a manager after a set time. The rules are covered in [invoice approval workflow thresholds and controls](/blog/invoice-approval-workflow-thresholds-controls).
3. **Month-end reporting.** Pull figures from accounting and spreadsheets into a standard report on a schedule, so the team reviews instead of assembling.

### People

4. **New hire setup.** When an offer is accepted, create accounts, order equipment, assign training and notify the manager. A checklist version is in [the employee onboarding automation workflow](/blog/employee-onboarding-automation-workflow-checklist).
5. **Offboarding.** When someone leaves, remove access, collect equipment and update records on the last day. The process is in [the offboarding SOP](/blog/employee-offboarding-sop-deprovisioning-assets-audit).

### IT and security

6. **Access requests.** Route requests to the right approver, grant access when approved and log the decision. See [the joiner, mover, leaver access workflow](/blog/joiner-mover-leaver-access-workflow-template).
7. **Alert triage.** Group monitoring alerts, suppress duplicates and notify the on-call person only when action is needed. See [automation monitoring and alerting](/blog/automation-monitoring-alerting-operations-teams).

### Customer support

8. **Ticket tagging and routing.** Label incoming requests by topic and urgency and send them to the right queue. A design for this is in [AI ticket triage for support operations](/blog/ai-ticket-triage-support-operations).
9. **Draft replies for review.** Prepare a suggested answer from your help articles, and let an agent approve or edit it before it is sent.

### Supply chain and procurement

10. **Reorder alerts.** Notify a buyer when stock falls below a set level, with the usual supplier and quantity filled in.
11. **Vendor onboarding.** Collect documents, run standard checks and create the vendor record once approved. See [the vendor onboarding SOP](/blog/vendor-onboarding-sop-procurement).

### Management

12. **Weekly operations digest.** Compile key numbers, overdue tasks and open incidents into a short message that arrives every Monday.

Notice what these have in common: each is **repeated often**, **follows rules** and **involves moving information between people or systems**. That is the profile to look for in your own business.

## What tools are involved

You do not need a large engineering team to begin. Operations teams commonly use:

- **No-code automation platforms** that connect apps with triggers and actions. See [Zapier vs Make vs n8n for operations teams](/blog/zapier-vs-make-vs-n8n-ops-teams).
- **Features inside tools you already own**, such as rules in your helpdesk, approval flows in your accounting system or automations in your project tool. These are often the cheapest place to start.
- **Spreadsheets and databases** as the place where data lives. See [Airtable, Notion and Smartsheet as an ops database](/blog/airtable-notion-smartsheet-ops-database).
- **APIs and webhooks** when two systems must talk to each other directly. See [webhooks, APIs and iPaaS integration patterns](/blog/webhooks-apis-ipaas-integration-patterns).
- **AI models** for reading documents or drafting text, added only to the steps that need them.

A useful rule is to use the simplest tool that does the job. Complexity is a cost you pay every time something changes.

## How to choose your first process

Good first candidates score well on four questions:

| Question | A good answer |
|---|---|
| How often does it happen? | Many times a week, so savings add up |
| Is it stable? | The steps have not changed in months |
| Is it rule-based? | Most cases follow the same path |
| What if it goes wrong? | A mistake is cheap to catch and fix |

Poor first candidates are processes that are unclear, change constantly or have no owner. Automating a messy process produces messy results faster. For a scoring method, see [what to automate first](/blog/what-to-automate-first-process-prioritization), and for a quick self-check, try the readiness scorecard below.

## A five-step way to start

### Step 1: Write down how the process works today

Describe the steps as they really happen, including the workarounds. Ask the people who do the work. A short written procedure, even a rough one, reveals steps nobody needs and decisions nobody wrote down. See [how to write an SOP people follow](/blog/how-to-write-an-sop-people-follow).

### Step 2: Measure the baseline

Before you change anything, record how many items go through, how long each takes, how many mistakes need fixing and how many people are involved. Without a baseline you will not be able to show that the automation helped. The [ROI calculator](/tools/roi-calculator) can turn these into hours and payback time.

### Step 3: Automate the simple path only

Most processes have a common route that covers most cases, and a long tail of exceptions. Automate the common route. Send everything else to a person with the context already attached. Trying to automate every exception is how projects stall.

### Step 4: Keep a person responsible

Every automation needs a named owner who watches it, handles exceptions and decides when to change it. Add a visible failure path: when something breaks, a person is told, and nothing is silently lost. Where decisions affect money, access or people, keep an approval step, as described in [human-in-the-loop design](/blog/human-in-the-loop-ai-approvals).

### Step 5: Review, then expand

Run it for a few weeks, compare against your baseline and fix the most common problems. Then pick the next process. Each automation should make the next one easier, because the documentation, owners and habits already exist. To keep a running automation healthy, use a [weekly and monthly review cadence](/blog/automation-alert-review-cadence-weekly-monthly).

## Rules, AI or a person: how to decide

Not every step needs the same kind of help. A quick way to choose is to ask what the step needs.

| The step needs | Best fit | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The same decision every time, based on clear fields | **Rules** | Route an invoice under a set amount to the budget owner |
| Reading messy text or documents, then a standard action | **AI, with a check** | Pull the vendor name and total from an emailed invoice, then confirm against the purchase order |
| Judgment, trade-offs or accountability | **A person** | Approve an unusual contract term or a large exception |
| A mix of the above | **Rules first, AI where needed, a person for exceptions** | Most real processes |

Start with rules. They are cheap, predictable and easy to explain. Add AI only to the steps where the input is unstructured, and always keep a way for a person to see what the automation did and why.

## A worked example: purchase request approvals

Imagine a team where staff ask for equipment and software by email. A manager reads each message, asks for missing details, finds the budget owner and chases the approval. It works, but requests get lost and nobody can say how long approvals take.

Here is how the five steps apply, with illustrative numbers.

1. **Write it down.** The team lists the steps: request, check budget, approve, order, confirm delivery. They discover that two approvals exist only because of a rule that changed two years ago.
2. **Measure.** For a month they count requests, time to approval and requests that needed follow-up questions. Suppose they find 60 requests a month, three days to approve on average and one in three needing a follow-up.
3. **Automate the simple path.** A short form replaces the email and makes the missing fields required. A rule sends requests under a set amount to the budget owner and sends larger ones to the department head. A reminder goes out after one working day.
4. **Keep a person responsible.** The operations lead owns the workflow, reviews requests that fail the form checks and watches a simple list of items waiting longer than expected.
5. **Review.** After a month they compare against the baseline. Fewer follow-up questions and a shorter wait are the signs it works. If not, they change one rule and measure again.

The point of the example is the order of work: describe, measure, simplify, then automate. The tool comes last.

## Questions teams ask before they start

**Will automation replace our team?** Automation removes repetitive steps, not the need for people who understand the process. In practice the work shifts toward exceptions, quality checks and improvement. Tell the team early what will change and what will not.

**Do we need to buy something new?** Often not. Check what your accounting, helpdesk, project and HR tools already offer before adding another platform.

**How much will it save?** That depends on volume and how long the task takes today. Use your own numbers in the [ROI calculator](/tools/roi-calculator) instead of a figure from someone else's case study.

**What if it makes a mistake?** Design for it. Keep a log of what the automation did, a visible place for failed items and a person who can pause it. Start with low-risk steps, and add approvals where money, access or people are affected.

**How long does a first project take?** A small, well-chosen automation can often be built and tested in days or a few weeks. Writing down the process and agreeing the rules usually takes more time than building the workflow.

## What can go wrong

- **Automating a broken process.** The software will do the wrong thing quickly. Fix the process first.
- **No owner.** Automations nobody owns fail quietly, and the team goes back to doing it by hand.
- **Silent failures.** If an automation stops, someone must be told. Add alerts and a place where failed items land.
- **Duplicate actions.** A retried step that sends two payments or two emails causes real harm. Design steps so that repeating them is safe.
- **Too much too soon.** Big projects stall. Small automations that ship in a week build trust and momentum.
- **Ignoring security and data.** Give automations the minimum access they need, and be careful about sending sensitive data to outside services. See [how to audit AI tools and automations](/blog/how-to-audit-ai-tools-and-automations).
- **Counting only the build cost.** Maintenance, monitoring and review take time too. Include them when you calculate the return.

## Build, buy or use what you have

Many teams do not need to build anything. Check what your current tools offer before you buy a new one, then consider a no-code platform for connections between tools. Custom development makes sense when the process is central to your business or the off-the-shelf options do not fit. A longer comparison is in [build vs buy for operations software](/blog/build-vs-buy-operations-software).

## Key takeaways

- Operations automation hands repeatable, rule-based work to software so people can focus on exceptions and decisions.
- Most useful automation needs rules and integrations, not AI. Add AI only to steps that involve language or documents.
- Good first projects are frequent, stable and low-risk. Avoid unclear or constantly changing processes.
- Document the process, measure a baseline, automate the simple path, keep a named owner and design visible failure paths.
- Review results against your baseline before you expand.
