Operations leaders do not need to write code, but they do need to understand how their systems connect. The reason is practical. Almost every operational failure that begins with "the data did not get to the other system" traces back to an integration decision: how two tools were connected, what happens when the connection fails and who knows about it.

Vendors and engineers throw around terms such as API, webhook, polling, iPaaS and middleware as if everyone shares the vocabulary. Many operators do not, and are left nodding along in meetings about decisions that will shape their team's daily work.

This guide explains these ideas in plain language, then moves to what really matters: the integration patterns you can choose from, how to match them to your needs, and the design habits that keep connections reliable. It pairs well with our pieces on [choosing a system of record](/blog/system-of-record-source-of-truth) and [comparing automation platforms](/blog/zapier-vs-make-vs-n8n-ops-teams).

## The vocabulary, without the jargon

**API (application programming interface).** A defined way for one piece of software to ask another for something or to tell it to do something. Think of it as a menu and an ordering system. The menu lists what you can request, such as "get this customer" or "create this invoice", and the system takes your order in a structured format and returns a structured answer.

**Endpoint.** A specific address on an API where a particular kind of request goes, for example the place you send a request to get a list of orders.

**Request and response.** You send a request, such as "give me order 1234". The system sends back a response, such as the order details, or an error.

**Authentication.** Proof that you are allowed to ask. Common methods include API keys, which are like a password for software, and OAuth, which lets one app act on your behalf without handling your password.

**Webhook.** A message that a system sends to you when something happens, rather than waiting for you to ask. When a customer pays an invoice, the billing system can immediately notify another system. It is like a doorbell instead of repeatedly opening the door to check.

**Polling.** Checking repeatedly whether something has changed, for example asking every five minutes "any new orders?". Simple but wasteful, and it adds delay.

**Rate limit.** A cap on how many requests you can make in a period. Exceed it and the system temporarily refuses you.

**Payload.** The data contained in a request or a webhook message.

**JSON.** A common text format for structured data, readable by both people and software.

**iPaaS (integration platform as a service).** A hosted platform for building and running integrations, usually with a visual builder, prebuilt connectors, scheduling and monitoring. Tools such as Zapier, Make, n8n and enterprise offerings fall in or near this category.

**Middleware.** Software that sits between systems and moves, translates or coordinates data. An iPaaS is one kind.

**Connector.** A prebuilt integration with a specific app that handles authentication and common actions so you do not have to.

If you remember one distinction, remember this: an API is how you ask, and a webhook is how you get told.

## The main ways systems connect

Most integrations fall into a few families. Knowing them lets you choose deliberately.

### 1. Native integrations

Many tools offer built-in connections to other popular tools. You switch it on, log in to both and map a few settings.

**Strengths:** quick to set up, supported by the vendor and often maintained as the products change.

**Weaknesses:** limited flexibility, sometimes shallow in what they sync, and often fixed in direction and timing. You may not control field mapping or error handling.

**Best for:** standard, well-trodden connections such as syncing contacts between a CRM and a marketing tool, where the built-in behavior matches what you need.

### 2. iPaaS and automation platforms

You build the integration visually, defining a trigger, a sequence of steps and the logic between them.

**Strengths:** flexible, fast to build, usable by non-engineers, with logging and monitoring.

**Weaknesses:** costs scale with volume, complex logic can become hard to read, and you depend on the platform's reliability and limits.

**Best for:** connecting several systems, transforming data between them and automating handoffs across teams.

### 3. Direct API integration, built in-house or by a developer

A developer writes code that calls the APIs directly.

**Strengths:** maximum control, optimized for your exact needs, and no per-run platform fees.

**Weaknesses:** needs engineering time to build and maintain, and it must be monitored and updated when APIs change.

**Best for:** high volume, core business logic, unusual requirements or situations where reliability and performance are critical.

### 4. File-based integration

Systems exchange files, such as CSV exports placed on a secure server on a schedule.

**Strengths:** common in finance, banking, logistics and legacy systems. It is simple, and easy to audit.

**Weaknesses:** batch-oriented, slow, prone to format issues and easy to break when someone changes a column.

**Best for:** legacy systems, bulk transfers and partners who only support files.

### 5. Data warehouse and reverse sync

Data is collected from many systems into a central warehouse for analysis. Some teams then push cleaned or computed data back into operational tools.

**Strengths:** strong for reporting and for combining data across systems.

**Weaknesses:** introduces another system to own, and pushing data back raises ownership and conflict questions.

**Best for:** analytics, and carefully chosen syncs where the warehouse legitimately computes the value.

### 6. Manual transfer

A person copies and pastes. It is an integration too, and often the real one.

**Strengths:** requires no setup.

**Weaknesses:** slow, error-prone and invisible. It rarely scales.

Treat every manual step as a candidate for automation, but also as a source of insight into what the process actually is.

## Webhooks versus polling: choosing how to be notified

A very common design choice is how one system learns that something has changed in another.

| | Webhooks (event-driven) | Polling (scheduled checks) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timeliness | Near real time | Delayed by the polling interval |
| Efficiency | Runs only when something happens | Runs on every check, even if nothing changed |
| Cost on usage-priced platforms | Usually lower | Can be high, especially with short intervals |
| Reliability | Depends on delivery and your ability to receive it | Simple, and easy to retry |
| Complexity | Requires an endpoint to receive messages | Easy to start |
| Risk of missed events | Possible if delivery fails and is not retried | Lower, because you re-check the state |

A practical approach: prefer webhooks when the source supports them and timeliness matters, but add a safety net, such as a periodic reconciliation that checks for anything missed. Webhooks are not guaranteed to arrive, and good designs assume some will be lost or delivered twice.

## Patterns that make integrations dependable

Reliable integrations are built from a handful of habits.

### Idempotency: safe to run twice

An operation is **idempotent** if running it twice has the same effect as running it once. This matters because retries, duplicate webhooks and re-runs are normal. If "create invoice" runs twice, you may bill a customer twice. If "set status to paid" runs twice, nothing bad happens.

Design with this in mind. Use unique identifiers from the source system to detect repeats. Check whether a record already exists before creating it. Prefer "upsert" operations, which create a record if it does not exist and update it if it does.

### Retries with back-off

Temporary failures are common: a timeout, a brief outage, a rate limit. Automatically retry a few times, waiting longer between attempts. Do not retry forever, and do not retry in a way that makes a problem worse by flooding a struggling system.

### Dead letter queue or failure log

When an item cannot be processed after retries, do not discard it. Park it in a clear place, such as a sheet, a queue or a ticket, with the data and the error, so a person can fix and replay it. Silent loss is the worst outcome.

### Alerting that reaches humans

A failed integration nobody knows about is a hidden outage. Send alerts to a channel people actually watch, name an owner and make the alert say what failed, what data is affected and where to look. Avoid alert fatigue by alerting on meaningful conditions, such as repeated failures or growing backlogs, and not on every blip.

### Validation at the boundary

Check incoming data before acting on it. Required fields present, formats correct, values in expected ranges. Reject or quarantine bad data early, rather than letting it spread into downstream systems.

### Respecting rate limits

Know the limits of the systems you call. Batch requests where possible, space them out and handle "too many requests" responses by waiting. A burst job that works in testing can fail on a busy day.

### Versioning and change awareness

APIs change. Vendors add fields, retire endpoints and alter behavior. Subscribe to vendor change notices, keep track of which integrations depend on which endpoints, and test after updates. Prefer connectors and APIs with clear version policies.

### Secrets and access control

Keep credentials out of shared documents and screenshots. Use the least access needed, such as read-only where writing is unnecessary, and use dedicated service accounts instead of an individual's login, so that the integration does not break when that person leaves. Rotate credentials on a schedule and when staff change.

## A simple decision guide

Use these questions to choose an approach for a new integration.

1. **Does a native integration do exactly what you need?** If yes, start there. Check its limits on field mapping, direction and frequency.
2. **Is the logic multi-step, or does it involve more than two systems?** An iPaaS is likely a good fit.
3. **Is volume high, performance critical or the logic core to your product or finances?** Consider engineering involvement and a direct integration.
4. **Is the other side a legacy system or a partner that supports only files?** A file-based approach with careful validation may be your only option.
5. **Is timeliness important?** Prefer webhooks and events. If a daily batch is acceptable, a scheduled sync is simpler and cheaper.
6. **What is the cost of failure?** The higher it is, the more you should invest in monitoring, idempotency, review steps and testing.
7. **Who will maintain it?** The best design is one your team can actually support.

## Documenting an integration

Every connection that matters should have a short record. A one-page template does the job.

- **Name and purpose:** what business process it supports.
- **Systems and direction:** from where to where, and which system is the source of truth for the data.
- **Method:** native, iPaaS workflow, custom code or file.
- **Trigger and timing:** webhook, schedule or manual.
- **Data moved:** key fields and any transformations.
- **Owner and backup:** who maintains it and who covers when they are away.
- **Credentials:** which account it uses, and where it is stored.
- **Failure behavior:** what happens when it fails, who is alerted and how to replay.
- **Dependencies and limits:** rate limits, volume expectations and API versions.
- **Last reviewed:** date and by whom.

This documentation is cheap to write and extremely valuable the first time something breaks.

## Illustrative scenario: new customer onboarding

Imagine that a signed deal in the CRM should trigger several things: create the customer in the billing system, open an onboarding project, notify the customer success team and send a welcome email.

A resilient design might work like this. The CRM sends a webhook when a deal moves to "Closed Won". An automation workflow receives it, checks that the required fields are present and checks whether a billing customer already exists using the CRM's unique ID. If not, it creates one and stores the billing ID back on the CRM record. It then creates the project, posts a message to the team's channel and triggers the email. If any step fails, the workflow retries, then logs the failure with the deal ID to a monitored list and alerts the owner. A nightly check compares closed deals with billing customers and flags any mismatch.

None of this requires deep engineering. It requires thinking about duplicates, failures and visibility from the start.

## Common mistakes

**Assuming integrations just work.** They need owners, monitoring and maintenance.

**Polling too frequently.** It wastes money and hits rate limits without improving outcomes.

**Ignoring duplicates.** Retries and repeated webhooks will happen.

**Tying integrations to a personal login.** When that person leaves, everything stops.

**Building two-way syncs without clear ownership.** This invites overwrites and loops.

**No failure path.** If errors disappear silently, you will find them in an audit or from an angry customer.

**Undocumented spaghetti.** Integrations built ad hoc over years become impossible to change safely.

## Key takeaways

- An API is how one system asks another for something. A webhook is how a system tells you something happened.
- Choose among native integrations, iPaaS, direct API work, files and warehouse patterns based on complexity, volume, risk and who will maintain it.
- Prefer event-driven webhooks for timeliness, backed by a reconciliation check for anything missed.
- Build for failure: idempotency, retries with back-off, a failure log, useful alerts, validation and rate-limit awareness.
- Use service accounts, least privilege and documented ownership.
- Write a one-page record for every integration that matters.

Integrations are the nervous system of your operation. A little design thinking up front keeps signals flowing and tells you quickly when they stop.
