Every operations team eventually reaches the same point. A process that began as a spreadsheet and a few emails now crosses five tools, and somebody is spending hours each week copying data between them. The obvious fix is an automation platform, and three names come up in nearly every conversation: Zapier, Make and n8n.

All three connect apps, move data and run logic without a custom-built integration. They differ in philosophy, in who they suit and in how their costs and risks grow over time. Picking the wrong one rarely causes a dramatic failure. It causes a slow accumulation of workarounds, surprise bills and brittle workflows that only one person understands.

This guide is deliberately vendor-neutral. It does not crown a winner, because the right choice depends on your team, your volume and your tolerance for managing infrastructure. It gives you criteria, a comparison across the dimensions that matter and a way to test your decision before you commit. Features and pricing change often, so treat every specific below as a prompt to verify on the vendor's current pages.

## What these tools have in common

At a high level, all three work the same way. A **trigger** starts a run, such as a new row, an incoming webhook, a form submission or a schedule. A series of **actions** then call apps, transform data, branch on conditions and write results elsewhere. Each platform offers a library of prebuilt connectors, a generic way to call any API over HTTP, a visual builder and some form of run history and error handling.

Because the core idea is shared, the decision rarely comes down to whether a tool can do a task. It comes down to how comfortably it does it, who can maintain it and what happens as you grow.

## The three in character

These descriptions are generalizations. Confirm current details yourself.

**Zapier** is generally known for the largest catalog of app integrations and for being approachable to non-technical users. Its linear, step-by-step builder is easy to learn, and it is often the fastest path from idea to a working automation. Teams commonly choose it when speed, breadth of connectors and ease of use outweigh the need for complex logic.

**Make** is generally known for a visual canvas that makes branching, iteration and data transformation more explicit. Many teams find it powerful for multi-step scenarios with routing, loops and aggregation, at a steeper learning curve than a purely linear builder. It tends to appeal to operations specialists comfortable thinking about data structures.

**n8n** is generally known as a more developer-friendly, node-based tool that can be self-hosted as well as used as a hosted service. It offers deep flexibility, including custom code steps, and gives teams control over where data and workflows run. The trade-off is more responsibility, especially if you self-host. Check its license terms, because its licensing differs from traditional open source and affects some commercial uses.

If you recognize your team in one of these descriptions, good. But do not decide on character alone. Work through the criteria below.

## Criterion 1: who will build and maintain workflows

The most important question is not about the software. It is about people.

Ask who will create automations and who will fix them at 8 a.m. on a Monday when one breaks.

- If **business users** such as coordinators, analysts and managers will build most workflows, prioritize approachability, clear error messages and good documentation.
- If a small **operations or revops specialist** group will own them, a canvas that exposes data flow and supports complex logic may serve you better.
- If **engineers** will be involved, they may prefer a tool with code steps, version control practices and the option to self-host.

A tool too complex for your builders will produce abandoned workflows. A tool too limited for your needs will push people into awkward workarounds. Match the tool to the real maintainers.

## Criterion 2: how pricing scales with your usage

Pricing is where teams are most often surprised. The platforms measure usage differently, and the unit of measure determines how your bill grows.

In general terms, some platforms bill per task or step executed, some bill per operation or credit consumed by individual module runs, and some bill per workflow execution regardless of how many steps it contains. Self-hosting changes the equation again, replacing a software bill with an infrastructure and maintenance cost. Plans, limits and units change, so verify current pricing before you decide.

What to do in practice:

1. **List your top ten candidate workflows.**
2. **Estimate runs per month for each,** using real volumes from the systems involved.
3. **Count the steps in each workflow,** including filters, routers, lookups and error handlers.
4. **Calculate cost under each platform's current unit of billing.** Do it in a simple spreadsheet.
5. **Model growth.** Double the volume and see what happens. Then add the workflows you expect to build next year.

A workflow that loops over every line item in an order, or that polls an API every few minutes, can consume far more units than you expect. Polling-heavy designs are a classic source of bill shock. Prefer webhooks and event triggers where the source supports them.

Also account for the hidden costs: builder time, maintenance and the cost of failures. The cheapest platform on paper is not necessarily the cheapest in practice.

## Criterion 3: complexity of your logic

Most operations automations are simple: when X happens, create Y and notify Z. For these, all three tools work, and ease of use should dominate your decision.

Complexity creeps in with:

- **Branching and routing** across many conditions.
- **Loops and batching** over lists of records.
- **Data transformation,** such as reshaping JSON, parsing text and merging records from different sources.
- **Error handling,** including retries, fallbacks and routes for failed items.
- **Waiting and human steps,** like pausing for an approval.
- **Custom logic** that needs code.

Test each platform with your gnarliest realistic workflow, not a trivial one. Build the same moderately complex scenario in each and compare how it feels. Notice how easy it is to see the data at each step, how clearly errors are reported and how readable the finished workflow is to someone who did not build it.

## Criterion 4: error handling and reliability

Automations fail. APIs time out, rate limits hit, fields come back empty and credentials expire. The question is what happens next.

Evaluate each platform on these points.

- **Visibility.** Can you see every run, its inputs and outputs and exactly where it failed?
- **Retries.** Are automatic retries configurable, with sensible back-off?
- **Error routes.** Can you define what happens on failure, such as logging, alerting a person or taking an alternative path?
- **Replay.** Can you re-run a failed execution after fixing the cause, without duplicating side effects?
- **Alerting.** Will the right person hear about failures quickly, in the channel they actually watch?
- **Idempotency.** Can you design workflows so that running twice does not create duplicates? This is partly your design and partly the tool's support.

A platform that makes success easy and failure opaque will cost you far more than one with slightly fewer connectors.

## Criterion 5: governance, access control and collaboration

A tool used by one person is a tool. A tool used by a team becomes an operational system, and it needs governance.

Questions to ask:

- **Roles and permissions.** Can you separate who builds, who edits and who can only view?
- **Shared credentials.** Are connections owned by individuals, who may leave the company, or by shared service accounts? Workflows tied to one employee's login break when they depart.
- **Environments.** Can you test changes safely before they affect production?
- **Version history.** Can you see who changed what and roll back?
- **Audit logs.** Is there a record suitable for your compliance needs?
- **Folders and naming.** Can you organize dozens or hundreds of workflows sensibly?

If your company has a security or IT team, involve them early. Automations often hold powerful credentials to finance, HR and customer systems, which makes them part of your attack surface.

## Criterion 6: security, data residency and hosting

For many teams this criterion decides everything.

**Hosted services** are simple to start with and require little maintenance. Your data passes through the vendor's infrastructure, so review its security documentation, certifications, data retention policies and where data is processed.

**Self-hosting** gives you control over where data lives and which network it can reach. It can help with strict residency or internal-network requirements. It also makes you responsible for updates, backups, scaling, monitoring and security patches. Be honest about whether your team has the capacity. An unmaintained self-hosted automation server is a liability.

Questions to settle:

- Does any workflow handle regulated or sensitive data, such as personal, financial or health information?
- Do your customers or contracts restrict where data can be processed?
- How are credentials stored and encrypted?
- Does your IT policy require single sign-on or specific access controls?

If you are unsure, run the question past your security lead before building anything sensitive.

## Criterion 7: integrations you actually need

The size of a connector catalog matters less than whether the specific apps you use are well supported.

List your must-have systems: your CRM, ERP or accounting tool, ticketing system, HR system, data warehouse, messaging apps and any industry-specific software. For each, check:

- Is there a prebuilt connector, and does it cover the specific actions and triggers you need?
- How current and well maintained is it?
- If there is no connector, how easy is it to call the API directly using generic HTTP steps and handle authentication, pagination and rate limits?

Most mature teams end up using the generic HTTP capability more than they expect. A platform with excellent HTTP, authentication and data-handling tools can compensate for a missing connector. A platform that handles only the happy path of prebuilt connectors can leave you stuck.

## How the three meter your usage (checked October 11, 2026)

Pricing pages change often, so treat this as a snapshot and verify it. What matters more than the price tags is the **unit each platform charges for**, because it decides how your bill grows as workflows get longer or busier.

| | Zapier | Make | n8n Cloud |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What is metered | Tasks. Only successful action steps count. Triggers, filters and paths do not. | Credits. For most apps one operation is one credit. Some AI and advanced features use dynamic credits based on tokens, file size or run time. | Executions. One execution is one full workflow run, however many steps it has. |
| What drives cost | The number of action steps per run, times runs | The number of operations per run, times runs | The number of runs |
| Self-hosting | Not offered | Not offered | A self-hosted Community Edition exists. Read n8n's license terms before using it commercially. |

Sources: [Zapier Help](https://help.zapier.com/hc/en-us/articles/8496196837261-Tasks-in-Zapier), [Make Help](https://help.make.com/credits) and [n8n pricing](https://n8n.io/pricing/).

### An illustration, not a quote

Take one workflow with six action steps that runs 1,000 times a month, with every step succeeding. On a per-step meter such as Zapier tasks, that is about 6,000 billable units. On Make it is roughly 6,000 operations if each module counts as one. On n8n Cloud it is 1,000 executions. Now add four more steps for formatting and notifications. The first two meters grow by about 4,000. The third does not move.

That is why long, multi-step workflows tend to favor execution-based pricing, and why short, simple automations across many apps can be cheaper on a per-step meter at low volume. It also means the cheapest plan on the pricing page is rarely the cheapest for your workflows. Count your real steps and runs first.

On n8n's pricing page at the time of writing, Cloud plans were listed at about 20 euros a month for 2,500 executions (Starter) and about 50 euros a month for 10,000 executions (Pro), on annual billing, with unlimited users and workflows. Business-tier features on self-hosted instances require a license key. Check the current page before you decide.

## Run a two-week pilot

Evidence beats opinion. A short structured trial settles most disagreements.

1. **Choose three representative workflows.** One simple, one with branching and data transformation, and one that handles an error condition.
2. **Build each in every candidate platform.** Have the people who will actually maintain them do the building.
3. **Run them against real or realistic data** in a safe environment.
4. **Break them on purpose.** Send bad data, revoke a credential, hit a rate limit. Observe how the platform reports it.
5. **Hand each workflow to a colleague** who did not build it and ask them to explain and modify it. This tests maintainability better than anything else.
6. **Compare the cost** at projected volume using real usage numbers from the trial.
7. **Review with security and IT** for the finalists.

Score each platform against your weighted criteria and decide. Write down the reasons so that you can revisit the decision when circumstances change.

## Common scenarios and tendencies

These are tendencies, not rules.

- **A small team that needs quick wins across many SaaS apps, with little technical staff:** an approachable tool with a broad catalog often fits best, provided volume keeps costs reasonable.
- **An operations specialist team building complex, data-heavy scenarios:** a visual canvas that exposes branching and data structures often fits well.
- **A team with engineering support, high volume, data residency needs or a desire for custom code:** a flexible, self-hostable platform deserves serious consideration, as long as someone owns its upkeep.
- **A growing company with all of the above:** it is common to use more than one tool. If you do, agree on boundaries and document which workflows live where, or you will create a maintenance maze.

## Avoiding lock-in and technical debt

Whichever you choose, a few habits protect you.

**Document each workflow.** Keep a short description of its purpose, trigger, systems touched, owner and what to do when it fails. Store it where the team can find it.

**Name things clearly.** "New deal to onboarding task" is far better than "Untitled scenario 14".

**Keep business logic out of the middle where you can.** Complex rules scattered across dozens of workflows are hard to change. Consider keeping critical rules in a system of record or a shared configuration.

**Limit single points of failure.** No workflow should depend on one person's credentials or one person's memory.

**Review regularly.** Schedule a quarterly pass to retire unused workflows, check failure rates and recheck costs.

**Plan for exit.** Know how you would export or rebuild your workflows if you had to switch. You will probably never need to, but thinking it through improves your design.

This is also where the broader question of your stack matters. ## Limits that cause quiet failures

Feature lists rarely mention the edges where a workflow stops working. These are limits stated in each vendor's own documentation, retrieved on October 19, 2026. They change, so treat them as a prompt to read the current page, not as permanent facts.

| Platform | Limit in the documentation | What to design for |
|---|---|---|
| Make | A custom webhook queue holds 667 items per 10,000 licensed monthly credits, up to 10,000 items. When it is full, extra data is rejected with a "Queue is full" response. | A burst of events with a slow or stopped scenario can lose data. Alert on queue depth, and make senders retry. |
| Make | Up to 300 incoming requests per 10 seconds are processed. Requests beyond that return HTTP 429. | Senders that fire in bursts need back-off. |
| Make | A webhook not connected to any scenario for more than 5 days (120 hours) is deactivated and returns 410 Gone. | A paused or abandoned scenario can break the sender later. Remove or update the sender when you retire a scenario. |
| n8n | The maximum payload size defaults to 16 MiB (`N8N_PAYLOAD_SIZE_MAX`) on a self-hosted instance, and a restart is needed to change it. Higher values need more memory and CPU. | Large attachments sent as webhook bodies can be refused. Send a link to the file instead of the file. |
| Zapier | In the developer platform documentation, a REST hook trigger is limited to 10,000 webhooks per 5 minutes and 30 per second, and a 429 response is returned when a sender exceeds the limits. The same page gives a 30 second limit for requests and scripting in triggers and actions. | These figures describe app integrations built on Zapier's platform. Check the limits for the exact trigger you use, and make senders retry on 429. |

Zapier's own guidance for senders that hit a limit is to support a retry and back-off schedule so the data is eventually received. That is good advice for every platform in the table, and it is the reason to design every workflow to be safe to run twice. See the [idempotency section](/blog/webhooks-apis-ipaas-integration-patterns) of our integration guide.

For your own team, the practical step is small. Before you commit to a platform, write down the largest payload, the busiest burst and the longest step you expect, and check each against the current documentation.

## Key takeaways

- All three platforms can automate most operations tasks. The decision is about fit, not capability.
- Start with people: who builds, who maintains and who gets paged when something breaks.
- Understand each platform's billing unit and model your real volume, including growth and polling.
- Judge error handling, replay and visibility as seriously as features.
- Treat governance, security and hosting as first-class criteria, especially for sensitive data.
- Verify current features and pricing yourself, then run a two-week pilot with real workflows before committing.

The best automation platform is the one your team will actually maintain, at a cost you can predict, with failures you can see and fix.
